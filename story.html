Our Story

Legacy.

Four decades, three generations, and one room on East Georgia Street. This is how the family got here.

Cambodia

A table set in Phnom Penh

Before Phnom Penh was a restaurant in Vancouver, it was home. A family grandfather was a well known chef there, and a family grandmother had recipes of her own. The crab roll and the garlic squid on the menu today came from them. None of it was written down. It was learned by watching, helping and remembering.

1975

Stay together

In 1975 the Khmer Rouge emptied the city and the family left on foot for Vietnam. The walk took about a month and a half, much of it through jungle. One sister was missing for close to ten hours. Their mother repeated the same instruction the whole way. Stay together. It was about survival then. It became the way the family runs a restaurant.

Vietnam

Noodles on a street

Vietnam meant starting over with nothing. The family sold Cambodian style noodles on the street, and eventually opened a small noodle shop. A grandfather had cooked professionally in Cambodia. Years later, in a city none of them had seen, they would sell noodles again.

1979

From Malaysia to Vancouver

The eldest son left first, on a fishing boat bound for Malaysia, and reached Canada. A daughter went to an immigration office to translate for an uncle and asked when her brother might sponsor the rest. He mapped out the family, handed her the paperwork. Three months later, approval came. On December 7, 1979, the family landed in Vancouver. The Red Cross met them at the airport with winter clothing. Her mother took work as a seamstress, her father as a labourer.

The early 1980s

A kitchen for neighbours

After a year of labour work, their father decided the family needed something of its own. The answer was food. They cooked out of a small room in Chinatown, on the same block as the restaurant today but across the street. For about two years it fed the city's newly arrived Vietnamese, Cambodian and Laotian families. Then it could not stay open.

1985

Crossing East Georgia Street

The family borrowed money from friends, put in their savings, and opened a real restaurant on the other side of the street. Having crossed countries to reach Vancouver, they crossed a road. For the first two years nobody took a salary. Every dollar went back into the room and to the friends who had made it possible.

1986

Expo fills the room

Expo 86 brought the city to Chinatown and the dining room filled. Word moved past the families who had backed the restaurant from the start. When more space was needed, their father took over the closed coffee bar next door and worked on the build himself. The wooden wainscoting he installed has been kept through every renovation since.

The kitchen

Three cuisines, one family

The menu carries Cambodian, Vietnamese and Chinese cooking because all three are in the family. The early dishes included amok, dry noodles and the crab rolls from Cambodia. The chicken wings, butter beef and beef luc lac came later, and they are what people cross the city for now.

The 2000s

Choosing one room

For more than a decade the family ran a second Phnom Penh on Broadway. It did well. When the lease came up their mother wanted everyone in one place, so they closed it and came back to East Georgia Street. As a business decision it was unusual. As a family decision it was the only one available.

Today

Three generations

The founding generation has stepped back from the daily work. A family member is Executive Chef and his son is the third generation in the kitchen. The same thing has been happening at the tables. Customers who were brought here as children now bring children of their own.

244 East Georgia

The table is still set

Four decades later, the table is still set. The recipes came from Cambodia. The family came from Vietnam. The restaurant stands on East Georgia Street. Today, grandchildren work the kitchen the same way their grandparents taught them. By watching, helping, and remembering. This is Phnom Penh.
